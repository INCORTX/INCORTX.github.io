Assignments & Marking

How every assignment in this course is set, presented and marked. The same seven things are marked every session. The topic lists themselves live in each session's notebook, where you pick one and load its data.

Groups of three or four, named A–J. One topic per group, first come first served, and you keep that dataset for the rest of the course. Everybody presents every assignment — two of you in the room, the rest in the video.

01How an assignment works

Groups of three or four, named by a letter A–J. One topic per group, first come first served. You keep this dataset for the rest of the course.

Every session runs the same way:

45 min  the demo             watch, do not type along
60 min  your hour            your topic, the five steps below
60 min  everyone presents    4 minutes on screen, then questions

The five steps of your hour — these are also exactly what you present:

StepWhat has to exist at the end
1EDAa chart cell that has been run
2Insightone sentence saying what you found, not what the chart is
3Problemwhat is wrong with this job — the data, or the question
4Solutionwhat you did about it, and why that choice, inside a Pipeline
5Metricwhich metric, why it fits, and what baseline() scored

Training a model is a bonus, not a requirement. Sessions 4–6 mostly will not fit one into the hour, and that is expected. Finish it at home instead.

You are not required to get a good result. A model that loses to its own baseline scores just as well, as long as you can say why and what you would try next.

02What is marked — seven things

#What is markedWhat it takes
1Presenting in the sessionAll five steps above, each one opened on screen from your notebook. Talking about a chart without scrolling to it does not count. Beyond being complete, we look for one of: an insight that changed what you did next · a metric justified by your own data · doubt about your own result, said out loud.
2Answering questionsTwo minutes after you finish. The instructor asks about your code and the room asks about your work. Anyone in the group may be picked, and the mark is the group's — so everybody has to have read the whole notebook, not just their own part.
3Handing in every file on timeNotebook, video, slides and README, as files, by end of the day before the next session. See What to hand in below — this one is all or nothing.
4The video — fluent, and it analyses somethingSpeaking, not reading a script word by word. Audio clear, moving through the slides without stalling. Eight minutes is long enough to go deeper than the four you had in the room, so go deeper.
5The slides — visualisation that is worth looking atEvery chart titled, with labelled axes and a caption saying what it shows. At least one concrete example — a real row, or a case the model got wrong. Text readable when watched in the video.
6The homework checklist, completeThe notebook finished: model trained, results with the baseline beside them, at least two settings compared, an error analysis, and three limitations that could only be written about your dataset.
7Length and durationSlides: 15 pages or more. Video: 8 minutes or more. Checked in ten seconds, and free to lose.
The mark belongs to the group and everybody gets the same one, questions included. The instructor picks who answers, so everyone has to understand the group's code — you cannot split the work four ways and never read the other three parts.

03Presenting in the session

Four minutes, then two minutes of questions. No slides for this round — open the notebook and scroll.

Two of you speak and hand over in the middle. Speaker 1 has what the data says, speaker 2 has what we did about it. The rest of your group presents the same work in the homework video, so everybody presents every assignment.

It adds to 240 seconds with nothing spare. Scroll the notebook to your starting cell before you go up: hunting for a cell live costs 20–30 seconds, and ten groups doing that is the whole hour.

04Answering questions about your code

The instructor points at one of you and asks one question about your own code. About forty seconds, so the question is short and the answer should be too. Questions come in two kinds.

Kind 1 — here is a line, what does it do?

  • what does this line do, and what breaks if I delete it?
  • why median and not mean? (tie it to your own chart)
  • what is the difference between fit and transform?
  • how many steps in your Pipeline, and what is each one fitted on?
  • your baseline() printed accuracy and F1 far apart — why?

Kind 2 — here is a thing, find it in your code.

  • show me the line that deals with missing values
  • which line splits train and test?
  • that number on screen — which line produced it?
  • where would you change it to use a RandomForest?
  • you dropped a column: which command, and why?
Kind 2 is the one that separates groups. If AI wrote your notebook you can usually still answer kind 1, because AI explains lines too. Scrolling to the line that handles missing values needs you to have read your own file, and ten seconds of hunting is visible from the back of the room.

Spend two minutes at the end of your hour asking each other these. It is the cheapest mark here.

An honest "we did not check that, and here is how we would find out" beats a confident answer that turns out to be wrong. Bluffing is the fastest way to lose this.

05Questions you can prepare for

Prepare for these. They are not meant to be a guessing game.

  • What does this line do, and what changes if I delete it?
  • Why did you fill missing values that way? What happens with the other method?
  • How many steps are in your Pipeline, and what data is each one fitted on?
  • What did baseline() print, and what does that number mean for your problem?
  • Which line of code produced the number on this slide?
  • If we cared more about recall than precision, what would you change?
  • Your baseline and your model are close — what would convince you the model is worth keeping?
  • Which of your three limitations would you fix first, and how?

06Asking questions — the audience earns too

Questions do not come only from the instructor. The other groups ask too, and asking earns marks that sit outside your assignment mark — they count towards participation.

Each presenting group takes 3–5 questions, and the first hand up gets the floor.

The questionWorth
Changes what that group should do next — spots a flaw they missed, asks whether the baseline used the right metric, names a limitation they skippedthe most
Makes something clearer for the whole room — why that k, show us the cell that number came fromsome
Asked to be seen asking · already answered in their four minutes · or a question you could ask any group without listeningnothing

That last line is the filter. "Why not try another model?" fits every group, which proves you listened to none of them.

Limits, so the marks spread around the room: capped per person per session · one question per presenting group · you cannot earn these while your own group is presenting.

Being fast gets you the microphone. It does not get you the mark. Write your question down while they are still talking — that is where the good ones come from.

07What to hand in

### Everything is a file. Links are not accepted.

No YouTube, no Google Drive, no Google Slides, no Colab link, no file-sharing site.

WhatFormat
Notebook.ipynb — in Colab use File → Download → Download .ipynb
Video.mp4 (or .mov)
Slides.pdf or .pptx
README.md or .txt

Why — a link can be edited after the deadline and a file cannot; links go dead or turn private and then there is nothing to mark; a file is a record.

Submitted through Google Classroom, one session at a time. Attach the file: Add → File, an upload. **Not Add → Link, and not a Drive file you still own** — both of those can be edited after the deadline, which is the thing this rule exists to stop.

At the end of the session hand in the notebook file itself. Not the Colab link — that is what makes "frozen when the hour ends" mean something.

Homework is due by the end of the day before the next session, and every session is the same: the notebook, the video, the slides and a README. All six.

Size is not a problem: a recording of slides with a voice over is almost all still frames, so eight minutes at 720p comes out around 30 MB.

The README says: where the data came from · anything you took from elsewhere (Kaggle, GitHub, a blog) · and where you used AI.

09AI and attribution

You may use AI to help write code, but you must understand every line you hand in — that is what the questions check. Say where you used it in the README. A group that writes "AI wrote this part and we read through it until we understood it" loses nothing.

If you take code or a notebook from anywhere (Kaggle, GitHub, a blog post), cite it on your slides and in the README. Not saying so counts as copying.

About Kaggle: several topics have ready-made notebooks online. You may read them, but cite them, and the baseline and the limitations have to be your own — because almost none of those notebooks have either.
