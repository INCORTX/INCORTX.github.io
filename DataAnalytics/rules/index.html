01How a session runs›Present last week’s work, watch the demo, then an hour on your own topic.

Groups of three or four, named by a letter A–J. One topic per group, first come first served.

Every session has its own topic list and you pick again each time — 12 to 15 topics per session, so ten groups never collide. The list for the next session comes out a week ahead, and you are expected to arrive with the data already loaded (session 1 is the exception: you pick in the room).

Every session runs the same way:

60 min  everyone presents    LAST session's work -- 5+ minutes each, then questions
45 min  the demo             watch, do not type along
60 min  your hour            your topic, the five steps below

The presentation hour is 60 minutes for the whole class, however many groups there are. Your share of it is announced on the day; the part that never changes is that you speak for at least five minutes and then take questions.

You present a session late, on purpose. The work you do in the hour is handed in that day as a record, then finished at home, handed in two days before the next session, and presented at the start of that next session from the file you handed in. It gives you a week to make something worth four minutes instead of forty seconds.

  • Session 1 has no presentation hour — nothing has been assigned yet — so its work block is 120 minutes instead of 60.
  • Assignment 6 is the exception, because there is no session 7: you present it at the end of session 6 itself, briefly — two minutes on whatever you have, then one question. The video, slides and notebook are then finished at home and handed in exactly like every other session.

The five steps of your hour — these are also exactly what you present, after one line of setup:

StepWhat has to exist at the end
0Questionwhat you are predicting, and what one row of this data is — one sentence, before anything else
1EDAa chart cell that has been run
2Insightone sentence saying what you found, not what the chart is
3Issuewhat is wrong with this job — the data, or the question. Not the business problem: the thing you found that gets in the way
4Solutionwhat you did about it, and why that choice, inside a Pipeline
5Metricwhich metric, why it fits, and what baseline() scored

Training a model is a bonus, not a requirement. Sessions 4–6 mostly will not fit one into the hour, and that is expected. Finish it at home instead.

You are not required to get a good result. A model that loses to its own baseline scores just as well, as long as you can say why and what you would try next.

03In the room›Two of you present for four minutes and take questions; asking good questions earns marks too.

This happens at the start of the next session, from the notebook you handed in the day before — not from whatever state your file was in when the work hour ended. It is already on the room's machine when your turn comes, so no laptops change hands.

Assignment 6 is the exception. There is no session after it, so you present it at the end of session 6 itself: two minutes, then one question, on whatever you reached in the work block. Nobody expects all five steps finished in 35 minutes — show what you have and say what you would do next. The hand-in is unchanged.

Four minutes, then two minutes of questions. No slides for this round — open the notebook and scroll. The slides are marked separately, from the file you hand in.

Your group sends two representatives, and they hand over in the middle. Speaker 1 has what the data says, speaker 2 has what we did about it. They also take the questions, so send people who have read the whole notebook.

Everybody still presents every assignment — in the video. Every member of the group speaks there, which is a separate requirement from this one and is not satisfied by having been in the room.

All five have to fit the time you are given, with nothing spare. Save the notebook scrolled to your starting cell when you hand it in: hunting for a cell live costs 20–30 seconds, and ten groups doing that is the whole hour.

Rehearse at home, with the video. The people speaking in the room and the people speaking in the video are covering the same work, so it is one rehearsal, not two.

Answering questions

The instructor points at one of you and asks one question about your own code. About forty seconds, so the question is short and the answer should be too. Questions come in two kinds.

Kind 1 — here is a line, what does it do?

  • what does this line do, and what breaks if I delete it?
  • why median and not mean? (tie it to your own chart)
  • what is the difference between fit and transform?
  • how many steps in your Pipeline, and what is each one fitted on?
  • your baseline() printed accuracy and F1 far apart — why?

Kind 2 — here is a thing, find it in your code.

  • show me the line that deals with missing values
  • which line splits train and test?
  • that number on screen — which line produced it?
  • where would you change it to use a RandomForest?
  • you dropped a column: which command, and why?
Kind 2 is the one that separates groups. If AI wrote your notebook you can usually still answer kind 1, because AI explains lines too. Scrolling to the line that handles missing values needs you to have read your own file, and ten seconds of hunting is visible from the back of the room.

Ask each other these while you prepare at home. It is the cheapest mark here, and you have a whole week to spend two minutes on it.

An honest "we did not check that, and here is how we would find out" beats a confident answer that turns out to be wrong. Bluffing is the fastest way to lose this.
Questions you can prepare for

Prepare for these. They are not meant to be a guessing game.

  • What does this line do, and what changes if I delete it?
  • Why did you fill missing values that way? What happens with the other method?
  • How many steps are in your Pipeline, and what data is each one fitted on?
  • What did baseline() print, and what does that number mean for your problem?
  • Which line of code produced the number on this slide?
  • If we cared more about recall than precision, what would you change?
  • Your baseline and your model are close — what would convince you the model is worth keeping?
  • Which of your three limitations would you fix first, and how?

Asking questions — the audience earns too

Questions do not come only from the instructor. The other groups ask too, and asking earns marks that sit outside your assignment mark — they count towards participation.

Each presenting group takes 3–5 questions, and the first hand up gets the floor.

The questionWorth
Changes what that group should do next — spots a flaw they missed, asks whether the baseline used the right metric, names a limitation they skippedthe most
Makes something clearer for the whole room — why that k, show us the cell that number came fromsome
Asked to be seen asking · already answered while they were speaking · or a question you could ask any group without listeningnothing

That last line is the filter. "Why not try another model?" fits every group, which proves you listened to none of them.

Limits, so the marks spread around the room: capped per person per session · one question per presenting group · you cannot earn these while your own group is presenting.

Being fast gets you the microphone. It does not get you the mark. Write your question down while they are still talking — that is where the good ones come from.
04What to hand in›Four files, named DA_ID_SLOT_Topic.ext, uploaded two days before the next session.

Everything is a file. Links are not accepted.

No YouTube, no Google Drive, no Google Slides, no Colab link, no file-sharing site — and no .zip or .rar. Upload each file on its own. The one link that is allowed is where the data comes from — a dataset URL or download script, written in the README.

WhatFormat
Notebook.ipynb — in Colab use File → Download → Download .ipynb
Video.mp4 (or .mov)
Slides.pdf or .pptx
README.md or .txt

Why — a link can be edited after the deadline and a file cannot; links go dead or turn private and then there is nothing to mark; a file is a record.

Submitted through Google Classroom, one session at a time. Attach the file: Add → File, an upload. Not Add → Link, and not a Drive file you still own — both of those can be edited after the deadline, which is the thing this rule exists to stop.

At the end of the session hand in the notebook file itself, as it stands. Not the Colab link — that is what makes "frozen when the hour ends" mean something. This one is a record that the hour happened; it is not marked for polish and it is not what you present.

Homework is due two days before the next session (the exact date is on Google Classroom), and every session is the same: the notebook, the video, the slides and a README. All six. The notebook in that hand-in is the one you put on screen at the start of the next session.

Size is not a problem: a recording of slides with a voice over is almost all still frames, so five minutes at 720p comes out under 20 MB.

The README says: where the data came from · anything you took from elsewhere (Kaggle, GitHub, a blog) · and where you used AI.

05The slide deck›Ten pages or more; eight of them are marked.

Ten pages minimum, and eight of the ten are pages we mark. Pages 1 and 2 are the context that makes the other eight readable.

PageWhat goes on it
1Title · group letter · members · TOPIC_ID · who presented in the room, who is speaking in the video
2The question you answered · what one row of the data is · where the data came from (a real link) · rows and columns · licence
3⭐ EDA — the chart, and what you found in it. Write what you saw, not what the chart is
4⭐ What is wrong with this data · where your topic's trap showed up
5⭐ What you did about it, and why that choice
6⭐ Which metric, and why it fits this problem · what baseline() scored and what that number means
7⭐ Results — the table, with the baseline beside it
8⭐ Two settings compared — what you changed, which way the result moved, and why
9⭐ Where your result is wrong — the form depends on the task, see the table below · three limitations
10⭐ Walk through 5–10 lines of your own code, line by line

Pages 3 to 6 are the five things you already presented in the room — EDA, insight, issue, solution, metric. Pages 7 to 10 are the four you had no time for.

Page 8 is smaller than it sounds

Two settings means two values of one thing. max_depth=4 against max_depth=8 is a complete answer. So is 3 clusters against 5, or with a column against without it. You do not need two different algorithms, and you are not being asked to tune anything.

setting          score
max_depth = 4    0.79
max_depth = 8    0.74   <- deeper is worse; it memorised the training rows

Two rows and one sentence saying which way it moved and why. The sentence is the part that counts — the two numbers on their own are not an analysis.

Page 9 — "wrong" means something different for each task

Not every task has a confusion matrix. Find your row:

Your taskWhat page 9 shows
Classificationconfusion matrix · 2–3 rows it got wrong, and what they have in common
Regressionresidual plot · the 2–3 rows it missed by the most
Clusteringpoints sitting between two clusters · a cluster with almost nothing in it · a cluster whose profile you cannot describe in words
Association rulesa rule with high confidence but lift near 1 · a rule that is true and useless
Autoencoderthe 5 worst reconstructions · which class has the highest reconstruction error
GANsamples that came out broken · repeated near-identical samples (mode collapse)
Reinforcement learningthe episodes that ended soonest · what state the agent was in when it failed

Every one of these is "show me a few specific cases and say what they have in common." That is the whole page. Three limitations go underneath.

More than ten pages is fine when you have something real to add — split the EDA across two pages, add what we would do with another week, add what surprised us. Padding to reach a number is obvious from the back of the room.

Every chart needs a title, labelled axes, and a caption saying what it shows, plus at least one concrete example somewhere in the deck: a real row, or a case the model got wrong. If the text cannot be read in the video, it does not count.

The deck describes the notebook you hand in, not the one you presented in class. If they differ, put one line on page 2 saying what changed after the session and why — that line is one of the best things you can put in this deck.
06AI and attribution›Allowed — if you understand every line and say where you used it.

You may use AI to help write code, but you must understand every line you hand in — that is what the questions check. Say where you used it in the README. A group that writes "AI wrote this part and we read through it until we understood it" loses nothing.

If you take code or a notebook from anywhere (Kaggle, GitHub, a blog post), cite it on your slides and in the README. Not saying so counts as copying.

About Kaggle: several topics have ready-made notebooks online. You may read them, but cite them, and the baseline and the limitations have to be your own — because almost none of those notebooks have either.
