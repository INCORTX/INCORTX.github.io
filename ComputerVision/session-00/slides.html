FP P12-03 (What is vision used for?) · Szeliski 2e ch. 1

Same “7”, new light — every number changes

  • Drag the light: you still read “7”, but every one of the 144 numbers changes.
  • Light, viewpoint, size, and other objects in front all change the numbers — not the object.
  • Your visual system handles this without effort; a program has to be taught to.

FP P12-04 (How do humans do it?) · TIF ch. 1, 3

In 1966, vision was planned as a summer project

“The summer vision project is an attempt to use our summer workers effectively in the construction of a significant part of a visual system.”
— Seymour Papert, MIT, 1966
  • The plan split vision into tasks for a group of MIT students — many of those tasks are still parts of computer vision today.
  • The task proved to be much harder than anybody expected. Sixty years later, this course is what we have learned since.

TIF §2.1

A whole vision system fits on one slide — if the world is simple

The blocks world (Larry Roberts, 1963): flat-sided objects resting on a white ground plane. Goal: recover the 3D scene from one image.

1 · ImageParallel (orthographic) projection turns the 3D blocks into a 2D image
→
2 · EdgesFind where brightness changes
→
3 · Edge typesIs the edge an occlusion, or a contact with the ground?
→
4 · SurfacesFigure vs ground; constraints propagate along edges
→
5 · 3DHeight of every point, inferred from the constraints

Every stage is hand-written for this toy world. Real scenes break these rules — so this course replaces hand rules with learning where it can, and keeps geometry where it must.

Course syllabus

Learning comes before geometry — on purpose

  1. Convolution leads straight into CNNs. The filters you design by hand in session 2 are what a CNN learns by itself in session 3.
  2. Your project will use pretrained models. Meeting CNNs, ViT and CLIP by week 5 gives you half a term to think about what to build.
  3. Geometry is what models do not learn for you. Cameras, matching and depth follow fixed rules — we study them once you have the learning tools to compare against.

Course syllabus · order follows Melbourne COMP90086 (ViT/CLIP before local features and stereo)

Course learning outcomes

By the end of the course you can:

  1. Explain how an image is formed and what information is lost
  2. Compute the core operations by hand — convolution, CNN shapes, attention scores, corners, transforms, depth, IoU, optical flow
  3. Use pretrained models (CNN, ViT, CLIP, YOLO, SAM) and explain what each one does
  4. Relate two images through features and geometry
  5. Evaluate a vision system with the right metric — and name its risks
  6. Design and compare two or three solutions to a real problem
