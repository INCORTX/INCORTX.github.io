Why vision is hard

Same “7”, new light — every number changes

  • Drag the light: you still read “7”, but all 144 numbers change.
  • Light, viewpoint, size and objects in front all change the numbers — not the object.
  • You handle this without effort; a program has to be taught to.

Nayar, First Principles of Computer Vision · Torralba et al. (2024), ch. 1 & 3

A little history

In 1966, vision was planned as a summer project

“The summer vision project is an attempt to use our summer workers effectively in the construction of a significant part of a visual system.”
— Seymour Papert, MIT, 1966
The planSplit vision into tasks for a group of MIT students — many of those tasks are still parts of computer vision today.
What happenedIt proved much harder than anybody expected. Sixty years later, this course is what we have learned since.

Quoted in Torralba et al. (2024), §2.1

Why this course exists
Every stage is hand-written for this toy world. Real scenes break these rules — so we replace hand rules with learning where we can, and keep geometry where we must.

Torralba et al. (2024), §2.2–2.6

Course design

Learning comes before geometry — on purpose

01Convolution → CNNThe filters you design by hand in session 2 are what a CNN learns by itself in session 3.
02Your project needs itMeeting CNNs, ViT and CLIP by session 4 gives you half a term to decide what to build.
03Geometry is not learned for youCameras, matching and depth follow fixed rules — we study them once you have learning to compare against.

Course outcomes

By the end of the course you can

  1. Explain how an image is formed and what information is lost
  2. Compute the core operations by hand — convolution, CNN shapes, attention scores, corners, transforms, depth, IoU, optical flow
  3. Use pretrained models (CNN, ViT, CLIP, YOLO, SAM) and explain what each one does
  4. Relate two images through features and geometry
  5. Evaluate a vision system with the right metric — and name its risks
  6. Design and compare two or three solutions to a real problem

Assignment

Five criteria for every presentation

  1. Right length & engaging — 10+ slides, 8+ minutes, at least one picture, no slide over ~6 lines
  2. Interesting answers — you answer questions from the class and the instructor well
  3. Fluent — you explain; you do not read the slides aloud, in the video or live
  4. Polished slides — pictures, equations, examples, little text, a clear flow, page numbers on every slide, and a title slide with names, student IDs and topic
  5. On time — video and slides one day before (timestamp)

Each criterion is scored on the course scale. Criteria 2, 3 and 5 are why copying an AI answer does not work.
